# SHS 1

## Lesson 1 — Number operations, fractions, ratio, percentages, and rates

### Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, learners should be able to calculate accurately with integers and fractions, simplify and share in a ratio, find a percentage of an amount, and solve a simple rate problem.

### Teach

Use the order of operations: brackets, powers, multiplication/division from left to right, then addition/subtraction from left to right. For fractions, find a common denominator before adding or subtracting. A ratio compares quantities in the same units; convert units before forming a ratio. A percentage is a number of parts per hundred. A rate compares quantities with different units, such as kilometres per hour or cedis per kilogram.

To share an amount in the ratio `a:b`, add the parts, divide the total by that sum, then multiply by each part. To find `p%` of an amount, calculate `p/100 × amount`.

### Worked examples

1. **Fraction addition:** `3/4 + 5/8`. Convert `3/4` to eighths: `6/8 + 5/8 = 11/8 = 1 3/8`.
2. **Ratio sharing:** Share GH₵560 in the ratio `3:5`. There are `3+5=8` parts; each part is `560÷8=70`. The shares are `3×70=GH₵210` and `5×70=GH₵350`.

### Guided activity

Ask pairs to compare two market prices, first converting both to the same unit (for example, price per kilogram). Have learners estimate before calculating and discuss whether the result is reasonable.

### Try work

1. Calculate `2/3 − 1/4`.
2. Share 72 mangoes in the ratio `5:4`.
3. Find 15% of GH₵240.
4. A trotro travels 150 km in 3 hours. Find its average speed.
5. A 5 kg bag of rice costs GH₵18 per kilogram. Find its total cost.

### Answer guide

1. `8/12 − 3/12 = 5/12`.
2. There are 9 parts, each worth 8 mangoes: 40 and 32.
3. `0.15 × 240 = GH₵36`.
4. `150÷3 = 50 km/h`.
5. `5×18 = GH₵90`.

## Lesson 2 — Sets and Venn diagrams

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to describe a set, use set notation, find union and intersection, find a complement in a stated universal set, and interpret a two-set Venn diagram.

### Teach

A set is a collection of distinct objects. `A ∪ B` means elements in A or B or both; `A ∩ B` means elements common to both. The complement `A'` contains the elements in the universal set `U` that are not in A. List each element once in a set. In a Venn diagram, place elements in the overlapping region first; then place elements belonging to only one set.

For finite sets, `n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)`. The overlap is subtracted because it was counted twice.

### Worked examples

1. Let `U={1,2,...,12}`, `A={2,4,6,8,10,12}` and `B={3,6,9,12}`. Then `A∩B={6,12}` and `A∪B={2,3,4,6,8,9,10,12}`.
2. In a class, 18 learners study French, 15 study Computing, and 7 study both. The number studying at least one is `18+15−7=26`.

### Guided activity

Survey classmates about two school clubs. Record the number in each club and both clubs, then draw and label a Venn diagram. Discuss why the overlap must be counted only once.

### Try work

1. If `A={a,e,i,o,u}` and `B={a,b,c,d,e}`, find `A∩B`.
2. For the sets in question 1, find `A∪B`.
3. If `U={1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8}` and `C={2,4,6,8}`, find `C'`.
4. In a survey, 24 learners like football, 17 like athletics, and 9 like both. How many like at least one sport?
5. If 32 learners were surveyed in question 4, how many like neither sport?

### Answer guide

1. `{a,e}`.
2. `{a,b,c,d,e,i,o,u}`.
3. `{1,3,5,7}`.
4. `24+17−9=32`.
5. `32−32=0`.

## Lesson 3 — Algebraic expressions and linear equations

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to collect like terms, use the distributive property, evaluate an expression, and solve and check a one-variable linear equation.

### Teach

An algebraic expression uses numbers, variables, and operations. Like terms have the same variable part and powers: `3x` and `−5x` are like terms, but `3x` and `3x²` are not. To expand brackets, multiply every term inside by the term outside. To solve an equation, perform the same operation on both sides until the variable is isolated. Check by substituting the answer into the original equation.

### Worked examples

1. **Simplify:** `4(2x−3)+x = 8x−12+x = 9x−12`.
2. **Solve:** `5(2x−1)=25`. Divide by 5: `2x−1=5`; add 1: `2x=6`; divide by 2: `x=3`. Check: `5(6−1)=25`.

### Guided activity

Let one learner describe a number puzzle (for example, “multiply a number by 3, then add 4”) while a partner writes an expression. Reverse the process by solving an equation.

### Try work

1. Simplify `7a−3a+2`.
2. Expand `3(2y+5)`.
3. Evaluate `2p²−q` when `p=3` and `q=4`.
4. Solve `4x+9=29`.
5. Solve `2(3m−4)=16`.

### Answer guide

1. `4a+2`.
2. `6y+15`.
3. `2(9)−4=14`.
4. `4x=20`, so `x=5`.
5. `3m−4=8`, so `3m=12` and `m=4`.

## Lesson 4 — Angles and plane shapes

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to use angle facts on a straight line, around a point, in triangles and quadrilaterals, and with parallel lines; identify basic properties of common plane shapes.

### Teach

Angles on a straight line total `180°`; angles around a point total `360°`; vertically opposite angles are equal. The interior angles of a triangle total `180°`, and those of a quadrilateral total `360°`. With parallel lines, corresponding and alternate angles are equal; co-interior angles total `180°`. Draw a clear diagram and mark known equal or supplementary angles before calculating.

### Worked examples

1. A triangle has angles `52°` and `61°`. Its third angle is `180−52−61 = 67°`.
2. Two co-interior angles are `3x°` and `2x°` on parallel lines. `3x+2x=180`, so `x=36`; the angles are `108°` and `72°`.

### Guided activity

Learners identify rectangles, triangles, and parallel lines in a classroom or compound. They sketch one example and label known angles or equal sides.

### Try work

1. Find the supplement of `128°`.
2. Find an angle around a point if the other angles are `90°`, `115°`, and `72°`.
3. A triangle has angles `x°`, `2x°`, and `3x°`. Find each angle.
4. A quadrilateral has three angles `80°`, `95°`, and `110°`. Find the fourth.
5. One angle in a pair of vertically opposite angles is `47°`. Find the other.

### Answer guide

1. `180−128=52°`.
2. `360−(90+115+72)=83°`.
3. `6x=180`, so `x=30°`; angles are `30°`, `60°`, `90°`.
4. `360−285=75°`.
5. `47°`.

## Lesson 5 — Measurement, perimeter, area, and volume

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to convert common metric units, calculate perimeter and area of rectangles and triangles, and find the volume of a cuboid using correct units.

### Teach

Choose units before calculating. For length, `1 km=1000 m` and `1 m=100 cm`. For area, square the conversion factor: `1 m²=10,000 cm²`. Perimeter is the distance around a 2-D shape. Rectangle area is `length×width`; triangle area is `1/2×base×perpendicular height`. Cuboid volume is `length×width×height`. Always include squared units for area and cubed units for volume.

### Worked examples

1. A rectangular garden is 12 m by 7 m. Perimeter is `2(12+7)=38 m`; area is `12×7=84 m²`.
2. A cuboid water tank is 2 m long, 1.5 m wide, and 1 m high. Volume is `2×1.5×1=3 m³`.

### Guided activity

Measure a desk or classroom using a tape measure. Estimate first, then calculate its perimeter and area; discuss why the chosen units matter.

### Try work

1. Convert 3.5 km to metres.
2. Convert 2.4 litres to cubic centimetres, using `1 litre=1000 cm³`.
3. Find the area of a triangle with base 10 cm and height 7 cm.
4. Find the perimeter of a square of side 8.5 m.
5. Find the volume of a cuboid measuring 6 cm by 4 cm by 5 cm.

### Answer guide

1. `3500 m`.
2. `2400 cm³`.
3. `1/2×10×7=35 cm²`.
4. `4×8.5=34 m`.
5. `6×4×5=120 cm³`.

## Lesson 6 — Tables, charts, averages, and range

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to organize a small data set, select an appropriate chart, calculate mean, median, mode, and range, and interpret what an average says about the data.

### Teach

A frequency table records how often values occur. A bar chart compares categories; bars have gaps. A line graph is useful for change over time. The mean is total divided by number of observations; the median is the middle after ordering; the mode is the most frequent value; the range is maximum minus minimum. An unusual extreme value can affect the mean more than the median.

### Worked examples

1. For `4, 6, 6, 8, 11`, the mean is `35÷5=7`, median is 6, mode is 6, and range is `11−4=7`.
2. A learner records books read over four weeks: 2, 4, 3, 5. A line graph is suitable for showing how the weekly total changes.

### Guided activity

Collect a small, anonymous class data set (for example, daily travel time in minutes). Display it in a frequency table and compare the mean and median.

### Try work

1. Find the mean of `5, 7, 8, 10`.
2. Find the median of `3, 9, 4, 7, 12`.
3. Find the mode and range of `2, 4, 4, 5, 9`.
4. Which is more suitable for comparing favourite sports: a bar chart or a line graph? Explain.
5. A data set has values `10, 11, 12, 12, 55`. Which is larger, the mean or median, and why?

### Answer guide

1. `30÷4=7.5`.
2. Ordered data: `3,4,7,9,12`; median `7`.
3. Mode `4`; range `9−2=7`.
4. Bar chart; sports are categories, not a time sequence.
5. Mean is larger because the unusually high value 55 pulls it upward; median is 12.

# SHS 2

## Lesson 7 — Indices and standard form

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to use index laws for multiplication, division, and powers of powers, interpret zero and negative indices, and express very large or small numbers in standard form.

### Teach

For the same non-zero base: `aᵐ×aⁿ=aᵐ⁺ⁿ`, `aᵐ÷aⁿ=aᵐ⁻ⁿ`, and `(aᵐ)ⁿ=aᵐⁿ`. Also `a⁰=1` for `a≠0` and `a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ`. Standard form is `A×10ⁿ`, where `1≤A<10` and `n` is an integer. When multiplying standard-form numbers, multiply the first parts and add the powers of 10; then rewrite the first part if it is outside the required range.

### Worked examples

1. `2³×2⁴÷2² = 2⁽³⁺⁴⁻²⁾ = 2⁵ = 32`.
2. `(3×10⁴)(2×10⁻²) = 6×10²`, already in standard form.

### Guided activity

Learners match equivalent expressions on cards, including `10⁻³`, `1/1000`, and `0.001`; ask them to justify each match with an index law.

### Try work

1. Simplify `5²×5³`.
2. Simplify `(x³)⁴`.
3. Write `0.00072` in standard form.
4. Calculate `(4×10⁵)÷(2×10²)`.
5. Evaluate `2⁻³` as a fraction.

### Answer guide

1. `5⁵=3125`.
2. `x¹²`.
3. `7.2×10⁻⁴`.
4. `(4÷2)×10⁽⁵⁻²⁾=2×10³`.
5. `1/2³=1/8`.

## Lesson 8 — Simultaneous equations and inequalities

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to solve two linear equations by elimination or substitution, solve a one-variable linear inequality, and represent simple solution sets on a number line.

### Teach

The solution to simultaneous equations must satisfy both equations. For elimination, multiply one or both equations to make one variable's coefficients equal or opposites, then add or subtract. For substitution, rearrange one equation and substitute into the other. Solve inequalities like equations, but reverse the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number. Check a simultaneous solution in both original equations.

### Worked examples

1. Solve `x+y=10` and `x−y=2`. Add equations: `2x=12`, so `x=6`; then `y=4`.
2. Solve `−2x+3<11`. Subtract 3: `−2x<8`. Divide by `−2` and reverse the sign: **`x>−4`**.

### Guided activity

Create two simple “ticket price” equations from a local event scenario. Let pairs solve for the price of each ticket type, then verify both totals.

### Try work

1. Solve `x+y=13`, `x−y=3`.
2. Solve `2a+b=11`, `a+b=7`.
3. Solve `4x+2≤18`.
4. Solve `−3y>12`.
5. A pen and a notebook cost GH₵14 together. Two pens and one notebook cost GH₵20. Find each price.

### Answer guide

1. Add equations: `2x=16`; `x=8`, `y=5`.
2. Subtract the second equation from the first: `a=4`; `b=3`.
3. `4x≤16`, so `x≤4`.
4. Divide by `−3` and reverse the sign: `y<−4`.
5. Let pen=`p`, notebook=`n`. `p+n=14`, `2p+n=20`; subtract to get `p=6`, then `n=8`. Pen GH₵6, notebook GH₵8.

## Lesson 9 — Functions and linear graphs

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to substitute values into a function, complete a table of values, plot a straight-line graph, and interpret its gradient and intercept.

### Teach

A function rule maps an input to an output. For `y=mx+c`, `m` is the gradient and `c` is the vertical intercept. The gradient measures vertical change divided by horizontal change: `m=(y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁)`. A table helps generate accurate points. Plot points using a consistent scale and draw a ruler-straight line when the relation is linear.

### Worked examples

1. If `f(x)=2x+1`, then `f(3)=2(3)+1=7`.
2. For `y=2x+1`, when `x=−1,0,2`, the `y` values are `−1,1,5`. Plot `(-1,-1)`, `(0,1)`, `(2,5)`; the line crosses the y-axis at 1 and has gradient 2.

### Guided activity

Plot a simple distance-time rule such as `d=4t` for `t=0,1,2,3`. Explain the meaning of the gradient in the context.

### Try work

1. If `f(x)=3x−2`, find `f(5)`.
2. Complete the table for `y=x+3` when `x=−2,0,2`.
3. Find the gradient between `(1,4)` and `(3,10)`.
4. State the gradient and y-intercept of `y=−2x+5`.
5. A taxi's fare is `C=8+3d`, where `d` is distance in kilometres and C is cedis. Find the fare for 6 km and explain the 8.

### Answer guide

1. `13`.
2. `y=1,3,5`.
3. `(10−4)/(3−1)=3`.
4. Gradient `−2`; y-intercept `5`.
5. `C=8+18=GH₵26`; 8 is the fixed starting charge.

## Lesson 10 — Similarity, scale, and scale drawings

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to recognize similar shapes, use corresponding side ratios, convert a map scale into a real distance, and distinguish between a length scale factor and an area scale factor.

### Teach

Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides. If the enlargement scale factor is `k`, each length is multiplied by `k`; each area is multiplied by `k²`. A scale such as `1:50,000` means 1 unit on the map represents 50,000 of the same units on the ground. Convert to a useful unit only after applying the scale.

### Worked examples

1. A map uses scale `1:50,000`. A road measures 3.2 cm on the map. Actual length is `3.2×50,000=160,000 cm=1,600 m=1.6 km`.
2. A similar triangle has a side of 4 cm corresponding to 6 cm on a larger triangle. The length scale factor is `6÷4=1.5`. If the smaller triangle's area is 12 cm², the larger area is `12×1.5²=27 cm²`.

### Guided activity

Use a classroom sketch at a chosen scale. Learners draw a desk or room, then use the scale to recover an actual length and check it with a ruler or tape.

### Try work

1. On a `1:100,000` map, two towns are 4.5 cm apart. Find the real distance in kilometres.
2. Corresponding sides are 5 cm and 15 cm. Find the enlargement scale factor.
3. A shape is enlarged by scale factor 3. By what factor does its area change?
4. Two similar rectangles have corresponding widths 6 cm and 9 cm. If the smaller length is 10 cm, find the larger length.
5. A drawing scale is `1:25`. A table is 3.2 cm long on the drawing. Find its actual length in centimetres.

### Answer guide

1. `4.5×100,000=450,000 cm=4.5 km`.
2. `15÷5=3`.
3. `3²=9`.
4. Length factor `9÷6=1.5`; larger length `10×1.5=15 cm`.
5. `3.2×25=80 cm`.

## Lesson 11 — Area, volume, and surface area

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to select and use formulas for common 2-D and 3-D shapes, distinguish surface area from volume, and give answers with appropriate units.

### Teach

Review rectangle, triangle, and parallelogram area. For a prism, volume is cross-sectional area multiplied by length. A cuboid's volume is `lwh` and surface area is `2(lw+lh+wh)`. A cylinder's volume is `πr²h` and curved surface area is `2πrh`; total surface area adds the two circular ends, giving `2πr²+2πrh`. Identify the radius, perpendicular height, and units before substituting. Use a calculator's π key or a stated approximation.

### Worked examples

1. A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 7 cm. Volume is `π×3²×7=63π≈197.9 cm³`.
2. A cuboid is 5 cm by 4 cm by 3 cm. Surface area is `2(5×4+5×3+4×3)=2(20+15+12)=94 cm²`.

### Guided activity

Bring clean cylindrical packaging and cuboid boxes. Learners measure dimensions, estimate which has greater capacity, then calculate volume.

### Try work

1. Find the volume of a cuboid measuring 8 cm by 5 cm by 4 cm.
2. Find the area of a parallelogram with base 12 cm and perpendicular height 6 cm.
3. A cylinder has radius 2 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume in terms of π.
4. Find the total surface area of a cube of side 4 cm.
5. Explain why a volume answer should not be written in square centimetres.

### Answer guide

1. `160 cm³`.
2. `12×6=72 cm²`.
3. `π×2²×10=40π cm³` (about `125.7 cm³`).
4. `6×4²=96 cm²`.
5. Volume measures three-dimensional space and uses cubic units; square units measure area.

## Lesson 12 — Probability and spread of data

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to calculate simple probabilities, use complementary events, interpret equally likely outcomes, and compare the spread of two small data sets using range and interquartile range.

### Teach

For equally likely outcomes, `P(event)=number of favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes`. A probability lies from 0 to 1. The complement rule is `P(not A)=1−P(A)`. For an ordered data set, the range is maximum minus minimum. The interquartile range is `Q3−Q1`; quartile conventions should be stated, especially for small or even-sized sets. Greater spread means values are less tightly clustered, not necessarily that the average is greater.

### Worked examples

1. A fair die is rolled. The probability of a number greater than 4 is `2/6=1/3`.
2. For `2,4,5,7,8,10,12`, the median is 7, lower half `2,4,5` gives `Q1=4`, and upper half `8,10,12` gives `Q3=10`. IQR is `10−4=6`.

### Guided activity

Use a coin or numbered cards to compare experimental results with theoretical probability. Repeat enough trials to discuss why small samples can vary.

### Try work

1. A bag has 3 red and 5 blue counters. Find the probability of choosing red.
2. For the same bag, find the probability of not choosing red.
3. A fair coin is tossed twice. List the possible outcomes and find the probability of two heads.
4. Find the range of `14, 9, 22, 16, 11`.
5. Two groups have the same mean score, but Group A has a smaller range. What does this tell you about Group A's scores?

### Answer guide

1. `3/8`.
2. `5/8` or `1−3/8`.
3. `HH, HT, TH, TT`; probability `1/4`.
4. `22−9=13`.
5. Group A's scores are less spread out; its learners' scores are more similar in value.

## Lesson 13 — Sequences and patterns

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to identify arithmetic and geometric sequences, find a common difference or ratio, and use a rule to find a specified term.

### Teach

An arithmetic sequence has a constant difference `d`. Its nth term is `aₙ=a₁+(n−1)d`. A geometric sequence has a constant ratio `r`; its nth term is `aₙ=a₁r⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾`. Check consecutive terms rather than guessing from the first two alone. A pattern may also be described recursively, where each term is found from an earlier term.

### Worked examples

1. For `5, 9, 13, 17, ...`, `d=4`. The 10th term is `5+(10−1)4=41`.
2. For `3, 6, 12, 24, ...`, `r=2`. The 6th term is `3×2⁵=96`.

### Guided activity

Learners describe a growing tile pattern using words, a table, and a term rule. Compare how an arithmetic pattern differs from a geometric one.

### Try work

1. Find the next two terms: `7, 12, 17, ...`.
2. Find the 15th term of `2, 5, 8, ...`.
3. State whether `4, 12, 36, ...` is arithmetic or geometric and give its ratio.
4. Find the 5th term of `2, 6, 18, ...`.
5. An arithmetic sequence has first term 11 and common difference −2. Find its 8th term.

### Answer guide

1. `22, 27`.
2. `a₁₅=2+14×3=44`.
3. Geometric; ratio 3.
4. `2×3⁴=162`.
5. `11+7(−2)=−3`.

## Lesson 14 — Coordinate geometry and transformations

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to find a gradient and midpoint, use a straight-line equation, and describe or perform translations, reflections, and rotations on a coordinate grid.

### Teach

For points `(x₁,y₁)` and `(x₂,y₂)`, gradient is `(y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁)` and midpoint is `((x₁+x₂)/2,(y₁+y₂)/2)`. In `y=mx+c`, the gradient is `m` and the y-intercept is `c`. A translation moves every point by the same vector. A reflection flips points over a mirror line. A rotation turns a shape about a stated centre through a stated angle and direction. Track vertices in order to avoid changing the shape.

### Worked examples

1. Between `(2,3)` and `(6,11)`, gradient is `(11−3)/(6−2)=8/4=2`; midpoint is `((2+6)/2,(3+11)/2)=(4,7)`.
2. Translate `A(1,2)` by vector `(-3,4)`: add coordinates to get `A'(-2,6)`.

### Guided activity

On graph paper, plot a triangle, translate it by a chosen vector, then check that corresponding side lengths and orientation stay unchanged.

### Try work

1. Find the gradient between `(1,2)` and `(5,10)`.
2. Find the midpoint of `(-2,4)` and `(6,8)`.
3. State the y-intercept of `y=3x−7`.
4. Translate `P(4,-1)` by vector `(2,5)`.
5. Reflect `(3,2)` in the x-axis.

### Answer guide

1. `(10−2)/(5−1)=2`.
2. `((−2+6)/2,(4+8)/2)=(2,6)`.
3. `(0,−7)`.
4. `(6,4)`.
5. `(3,−2)`.

## Lesson 15 — Trigonometry and bearings

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to select a trigonometric ratio for a right-angled triangle, calculate a missing side or angle, and interpret a three-figure bearing measured clockwise from north.

### Teach

For an acute angle in a right-angled triangle: `sin θ=opposite/hypotenuse`, `cos θ=adjacent/hypotenuse`, `tan θ=opposite/adjacent`. Label sides relative to the chosen angle, choose the ratio containing the known and unknown sides, and check calculator mode is degrees. A bearing is measured clockwise from north and written with three digits, for example `065°`. Sketch a north line at the starting point before resolving a bearing problem.

### Worked examples

1. A learner stands 20 m from a tree; the angle of elevation to the top is `30°`. Height above eye level is `20 tan 30° ≈ 11.55 m`.
2. A point is due east of a station. Its bearing from the station is `090°`. If a second point lies due south, its bearing is `180°`.

### Guided activity

Use a compass or a drawn north arrow in the school compound. Learners describe the bearing of a visible landmark and check that bearings are measured clockwise from north.

### Try work

1. In a right triangle, opposite=`6 cm` and hypotenuse=`10 cm`. Find `sin θ`.
2. A right triangle has adjacent=`8 cm` and opposite=`6 cm`. Find `tan θ`.
3. A ladder 5 m long makes an angle of `60°` with level ground. Find the vertical height reached, to 1 decimal place.
4. Write the bearing for a direction due west.
5. A boat travels 4 km due north, then 3 km due east. Find its straight-line distance from the starting point and its bearing, to the nearest degree.

### Answer guide

1. `6/10=0.6`.
2. `6/8=0.75`.
3. `5 sin 60°≈4.3 m`.
4. `270°`.
5. Distance `√(4²+3²)=5 km`. Angle east of north is `tan⁻¹(3/4)≈36.9°`, so bearing is about `037°`.

## Lesson 16 — Compound measures and financial mathematics

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to calculate speed, density, and other simple compound measures, distinguish simple from compound interest, and solve everyday financial problems with units.

### Teach

A compound measure combines quantities, for example speed=`distance÷time`, density=`mass÷volume`, and pressure=`force÷area`. Convert units before substitution. Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal: `I=Prt`, where `r` is the annual rate as a decimal and `t` is time in years. Compound interest is added to the balance each period: `A=P(1+r)ⁿ`. State whether the question asks for interest only or the total amount.

### Worked examples

1. A bus travels 180 km in 3 hours. Average speed is `180÷3=60 km/h`.
2. GH₵2,000 is invested at 10% compound interest per year for 2 years. `A=2000(1.10)²=GH₵2,420`; compound interest is `2420−2000=GH₵420`.

### Guided activity

Compare a simple-interest and compound-interest offer using the same principal, rate, and period. Discuss why the amounts begin to differ over time.

### Try work

1. A 300 km journey takes 5 hours. Find the average speed.
2. Find the density of a 600 g object with volume 200 cm³.
3. Find the simple interest on GH₵1,500 at 8% per year for 3 years.
4. Find the total amount for GH₵1,000 invested at 10% compound interest for 2 years.
5. A pump moves 240 litres of water in 6 minutes. Find its rate in litres per minute.

### Answer guide

1. `60 km/h`.
2. `600÷200=3 g/cm³`.
3. `I=1500×0.08×3=GH₵360`.
4. `A=1000(1.10)²=GH₵1,210`.
5. `240÷6=40 L/min`.

## Lesson 17 — Statistics and probability

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to interpret grouped or ungrouped data, select a suitable average, calculate simple combined probabilities, and explain how sample size and bias affect conclusions.

### Teach

Use the mean when all values matter and the data have no extreme distortion; the median is often more representative when there are outliers; the mode identifies the most common value or category. For two independent events, multiply probabilities for “and”; for mutually exclusive events, add probabilities for “or.” A sample should fairly represent the population. A small or biased sample can produce a misleading conclusion. Always state what a statistic describes and what it does not prove.

### Worked examples

1. A bag has 3 red and 2 blue counters. Two counters are drawn **with replacement**. Probability of red then blue is `(3/5)×(2/5)=6/25`.
2. Daily earnings are GH₵80, GH₵85, GH₵90, GH₵95, and GH₵300. The mean is GH₵130; the median is GH₵90. The high outlier makes the median a better description of a typical day.

### Guided activity

Compare two anonymized class data sets. Ask groups to choose a summary statistic and defend why it best represents the data.

### Try work

1. A bag has 4 green and 6 yellow counters. Find the probability of green on one draw.
2. A fair coin is tossed and a fair die is rolled. Find the probability of heads and a 6.
3. Find the median of `4, 8, 9, 12, 17`.
4. Which average is usually more affected by a very large outlier: mean or median?
5. A survey asks only members of the school football team whether students enjoy sports. Identify one source of bias.

### Answer guide

1. `4/10=2/5`.
2. `(1/2)×(1/6)=1/12`.
3. `9`.
4. Mean.
5. The sample excludes many students and over-represents people likely to enjoy sports.

## Lesson 18 — Mixed problem-solving and examination strategies

### Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to break a multi-step problem into smaller steps, select relevant methods, show a clear chain of working, estimate to check reasonableness, and manage time in a test.

### Teach

Read the whole question and identify what is given and what is required. Underline quantities and units. Choose a method before calculating; draw a diagram or table if useful. Keep full precision during working and round only when asked. Check whether the answer's size and unit make sense. In a timed paper, secure marks on questions you can solve, show working for method marks, and return to harder questions.

### Worked examples

1. **Discount and change:** A school buys 4 calculators at GH₵125 each. A 10% discount is applied to the total. Original total=`4×125=GH₵500`; discount=`0.10×500=GH₵50`; amount paid=`GH₵450`.
2. **Area and cost:** A rectangular plot is 18 m by 12 m. Fencing costs GH₵25 per metre. Perimeter=`2(18+12)=60 m`; total cost=`60×25=GH₵1,500`.

### Guided activity

In pairs, solve one multi-step question. One learner explains the plan while the other checks units and arithmetic. Switch roles for a second problem.

### Try work

1. Three exercise books cost GH₵18. At the same rate, how much do 8 books cost?
2. A phone costs GH₵2,400 before a 5% discount. Find the discount and sale price.
3. A rectangular field is 25 m by 16 m. Find its area and perimeter.
4. A car travels 210 km at an average speed of 70 km/h. How long does the journey take?
5. A class has 18 girls and 12 boys. What fraction of the class are girls? Give the answer in simplest form and as a percentage.

### Answer guide

1. One book costs `18÷3=GH₵6`; 8 cost `GH₵48`.
2. Discount=`0.05×2400=GH₵120`; sale price=`GH₵2,280`.
3. Area=`400 m²`; perimeter=`82 m`.
4. Time=`210÷70=3 hours`.
5. Total=`30`; fraction=`18/30=3/5`; percentage=`60%`.
